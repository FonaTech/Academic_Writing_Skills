# Titles, abstracts and paratext

This guide covers titles, abstracts, key points, highlights, table-of-contents (TOC) entries and graphical-abstract text for any field. Sentence-level rules are in [style-rules.md](style-rules.md), and number reporting is in [quantitative-reporting.md](quantitative-reporting.md). Examples use placeholders (⟨artifact⟩, ⟨mechanism⟩, ⟨metric⟩, ⟨value⟩, ⟨condition⟩).

## Rules

### Titles

1. **Write the title as a noun phrase that names the artifact or, for a review, the organising variable.** Claim-sentence titles are acceptable only where the venue uses them.
2. **Keep an article title to about 12 words, with at most two modifiers before the head noun and no praise word.** A praise word ("efficient", "robust", "high-performance") needs a measured referent in the paper.
3. **Frame a review title by its organising variable or an evaluative lens, and drop recency and genre frames** ("Recent advances in …", "A comprehensive review of …"). A subtitle helps when it states a thesis or a lens.

### Abstracts of every type

4. **Open with a requirement, a quantified pressure or a definition**, not a generic statement of importance.

### Research articles and letters

5. **Order the abstract in five moves:** context in one or two sentences, gap, "Here, we" pivot, results with numbers, one line of implication. Three or more context sentences waste the reader's attention.
6. **State the gap as a missing capability or a named failure of the incumbent, with its cause.** "⟨Incumbent⟩ suffers from ⟨A⟩ because ⟨cause⟩" is stronger than "remains challenging".
7. **Pivot with "Here, we" and an active verb.** Passive pivots ("Here, … are developed") are weaker.
8. **Give two to five headline numbers, each with a scale, a condition and a baseline.**
9. **Label the evidence level of each headline number in the same sentence**: measured, simulated from measured inputs, post-processing, projected or estimated.
10. **Check every abstract number and qualifier against the body.** Common mismatches: a value rounded differently in the abstract and the text; a result filed under the wrong task category; "1% less" for a difference of 0.86 percentage points; a ratio mixed with a count.
11. **If the title or the gap names a figure of merit, give its value in the abstract.**
12. **End on the result or on one named consequence**, not on a "milestone", "potential" or "pave the way" closer.

### Reviews, perspectives and tutorials

13. **State the organising criterion, lens or thesis in one sentence.** A list of covered topics is not enough.
14. **End a review abstract on one to three cross-study findings**, with magnitudes where the body supports them, and one forward statement (the decisive result the field is working toward).
15. **Write the abstract as a review of the field's progress.** Never describe the screening of the literature, library counts or evidence grading in the abstract.
16. **Perspectives state a thesis** ("we argue that …") and two or three reasons, then a prescription.

### Paratext

17. **Write key points as four to six declarative bullets of 20–35 words, one per major section, each stating a finding or a judgment.** The last bullet names the next decisive result.
18. **Make highlights, TOC text and graphical-abstract text add something the abstract lacks**, such as a named strategy, a comparison or a claim. Bullets that restate coverage add nothing. Elsevier-family highlights are 3–5 bullets of at most 85 characters.

## Templates

**A. Research-article abstract (6–8 sentences, 140–200 words, or the journal limit).**
- S1 states the requirement or pressure.
- S2 gives the gap ("However, ⟨incumbent⟩ ⟨fails at X⟩ because ⟨cause⟩").
- S3 pivots ("Here, we ⟨verb⟩ ⟨artifact⟩ that ⟨mechanism⟩").
- S4 gives the scale and the first measured result with its condition.
- S5 gives a second result against a named baseline.
- S6 gives the system-, population- or task-level result with its evidence label.
- S7 (optional) names one consequence.

Shape: *⟨Application⟩ needs ⟨property⟩ at ⟨scale⟩. ⟨Incumbent⟩ meets ⟨target A⟩, but ⟨failure⟩ within ⟨time or condition⟩ limits it to ⟨restricted use⟩. Here, we show that ⟨intervention⟩ stabilises ⟨property⟩ in ⟨artifact⟩. Across ⟨N⟩ ⟨units⟩, ⟨metric⟩ varied by ⟨value⟩ over ⟨duration⟩ under ⟨condition⟩. ⟨Second metric⟩ was ⟨value⟩, against ⟨value⟩ for ⟨named baseline⟩. In simulations using the measured ⟨inputs⟩, ⟨task metric⟩ reached ⟨value⟩.*

**B. Review abstract (5–7 sentences, 150–220 words).**
- S1 states the stakes.
- S2 gives the field's central problem (a trade-off, an inconsistency in terms or accounting, or a missing level of integration).
- S3 says "Here we review ⟨X⟩, organised by ⟨criterion⟩".
- S4 gives the coverage in body order.
- S5 states one to three cross-study findings.
- S6 names the decisive next results.

**C. Perspective abstract (4–6 sentences, 80–200 words).** Describe the field's situation, state the thesis ("we make the case that …") and give two or three reasons. Add a plain statement of maturity and end on a prescription.

**D. Titles.** An article title combines up to two modifiers, the artifact, an enabler ("based on", "from", "using") and the task, in 5–12 words. Shape: "⟨Modifier⟩ ⟨artifact⟩ using ⟨enabler⟩ for ⟨task⟩." A review title names the organising variable or lever and the field, optionally followed by a lens after a colon. Shape: "⟨Lever⟩ in ⟨field⟩: from ⟨stage A⟩ to ⟨stage B⟩."

**E. Paratext.**
- Key points (Nature Reviews type): 4–6 bullets of 20–35 words in the present tense, one per major section, the last naming the next decisive result.
- Highlights (Elsevier type): 3–5 findings of at most 85 characters each.
- TOC entry (Wiley or ACS type): two sentences of 40–60 words in total. Sentence 1 says what was done or reviewed; sentence 2 gives the comparison or the claim.
- Graphical-abstract text: one line that names the mechanism or the comparison shown.

## Anti-patterns and fixes

| Pattern | Before (constructed) | After |
|---|---|---|
| Recency frame | "Recent advances in ⟨field⟩" | "⟨Lever⟩ in ⟨field⟩: ⟨lens⟩" |
| Generic opening | "⟨Field⟩ has attracted tremendous attention." | "⟨Application⟩ needs ⟨property⟩ at ⟨scale⟩ [ref]." |
| Passive pivot | "Here, ⟨artifacts⟩ are developed." | "Here, we develop ⟨artifact⟩ that ⟨mechanism⟩." |
| Unlabelled simulation | "The system achieves ⟨value⟩ accuracy." | "In simulations using measured ⟨inputs⟩, the system reaches ⟨value⟩." |
| Coverage-only review abstract | "This review covers A, B, C and D." | "Organised by ⟨criterion⟩, this Review shows that ⟨finding⟩." |
| Screening narrative | "We screened ⟨N⟩ papers and classified their evidence." | "⟨Field⟩ has progressed from ⟨stage A⟩ to ⟨stage B⟩; ⟨finding⟩." |
| Stock closer | "This work paves the way for next-generation ⟨applications⟩." | "The approach would allow ⟨named consequence⟩ at ⟨scale⟩." |

## Venue differences

Nature-family journals use unstructured abstracts of about 150–200 words with a "Here we" pivot; Nature Reviews journals add Key points. Cell Press journals add highlights, an eTOC blurb and a graphical abstract. Elsevier journals usually ask for highlights. ACS and Wiley journals ask for a TOC graphic with short text. Clinical journals often require structured abstracts (Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions). IEEE abstracts are a single paragraph, often with index terms. Always check the author guide for limits.

## Checklist

1. The title names the artifact or organising variable, with no praise word and no recency frame.
2. The abstract opens on a requirement, pressure or definition.
3. Article abstracts have a gap with its cause, a "Here, we" pivot and two to five conditioned numbers with evidence labels.
4. Review abstracts state the organising criterion and one to three cross-study findings.
5. No screening or library narrative.
6. Every abstract number matches the body exactly.
7. Paratext adds something the abstract lacks and fits the venue's limits.
