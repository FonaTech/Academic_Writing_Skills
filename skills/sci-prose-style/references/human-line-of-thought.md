# Writing from a researcher's line of thought

Use this guide whenever you draft or rewrite a paragraph of scientific prose, in any genre and in either language. It describes how an experienced author reasons through a paragraph and what that reasoning leaves on the page. Use it as the requested drafting method from the first draft; its effect on detector scores and expert judgments has not been established here. The reasoning decides which sentences exist, their order and their links. The text never voices the reasoning: it has no questions, no musings and no remarks about the writing.

## 1. What makes generated prose recognisable

The following are editorial patterns to inspect in context. They do not establish authorship or reliably predict detector decisions.

| Regularity | What it looks like | What an expert does instead |
|---|---|---|
| Parameter inventory | three or more sentences in a row that each report a value, with no reason or comparison | keeps the one or two numbers that settle the point, puts each in a clause that says what it is compared with or what it means, and moves the rest to a table or a parenthesis |
| Even causal chain | a connective on nearly every sentence (so, because, then, therefore, thus; 因此、从而、进而) | lets the order of the sentences carry most causes and keeps a connective only where a reader would miss the link |
| Uniform rhythm | every sentence 18–22 words long, every paragraph four to six sentences | lets the content set the length: a long sentence for a mechanism with its conditions, a short one for a plain result |
| Template angle | background, method, result, significance and outlook stamped onto every paragraph | uses only the steps the material has |
| Counted overview | "Two of them … In the first … The remaining two …" | names each group by what separates it from the others |
| Aphoristic verdict | "Scale still mattered." "The gap remains." | attaches the judgement to its evidence and scope in one sentence |
| Voiced reasoning | rhetorical questions; embedded questions as openers ("How much ⟨X⟩ matters depends on ⟨Y⟩"); "the key is", "the question is" | states the dependence or the finding directly |
| Promotional frame | "a promising route", "a major boost", "paves the way" | says what was measured, and under which condition |
| Moral ending | each paragraph closes on an importance or outlook sentence | ends on the last relevant fact, which the next paragraph takes up |
| Table-of-contents overview | a section opening that lists what each subsection will do | names the shared basis of the subsections and how they divide it |

Careless fixes for one regularity create another. Adding a connective to every sentence of a parameter list turns an inventory into an even causal chain. Cutting every long sentence to the same length replaces one uniform rhythm with another. A rewrite removes a regularity without introducing a new one, which is why writing from the argument (section 2) works better than patching sentences.

Tables, reference lists and abbreviation lists are dense and formulaic by nature. Do not rewrite them for style; keep their explanatory notes short and plain (section 7).

## 2. How a researcher thinks through a paragraph

An expert writes a paragraph as a chain of needs. Each sentence exists because the one before it created a need: a cost, a gap, a puzzle or a consequence. Answer these questions privately before writing. None of them appears in the text as a question.

1. **What did the reader just learn, and what does it leave unresolved?** The first sentence picks up that point, stated as the situation.
2. **Who did something about it, and why that choice?** Put the reason in the same sentence as the action: a participle, a relative clause, or "because" or "since" where the link would otherwise be missed.
3. **What did they see?** Give the one or two numbers that settle the point, each inside a clause that says what it is compared with or what it implies.
4. **What does the result mean, and how far does it reach?** State the judgement as a plain claim with its basis and its scope. A concession belongs here ("although each run used a single ⟨condition⟩").
5. **What did the result leave open?** End on that fact. It becomes the opening of the next paragraph. A paragraph needs no forecast and no moral.

The voice is that of a colleague explaining the field: confident about what was shown, specific about limits, and silent about the writer's own deliberation.

## 3. What the line of thought looks like on the page

| Human pattern | What it replaces | Constructed example |
|---|---|---|
| A solution, its cost, then the next solution | parallel lists of options | "⟨Option A⟩ is faster because it runs in parallel. It draws more power, which rules it out for ⟨setting⟩. ⟨Option B⟩ draws less and sits between the two in speed." |
| A study told in order: situation, action, observation, interpretation | a result inventory under an author name | "In ⟨year⟩, ⟨A⟩ and colleagues replaced ⟨X⟩ with ⟨Y⟩ to avoid ⟨cost⟩. The error fell from ⟨v1⟩ to ⟨v2⟩, which they traced to ⟨cause⟩." |
| The actor as subject, carried forward | abstract or passive subjects ("It was demonstrated that …") | "⟨A⟩ et al. grew ⟨material⟩ on ⟨substrate⟩. The same team later …" |
| Cause carried by sentence order | a connective on every sentence | "⟨A⟩ et al. added ⟨X⟩ to the ⟨system⟩. The ⟨metric⟩ rose from ⟨v1⟩ to ⟨v2⟩ (⟨condition⟩)." |
| Groups named by what separates them | counted overviews | "Methods that store a state differ from methods that change in time. The first group holds ⟨quantity⟩ …" |
| Numbers inside reason or comparison clauses | bare parameter sentences | "The ⟨system⟩ held ⟨n⟩ states, enough for ⟨task⟩ but only under ⟨condition⟩." |
| Conditions in parentheses | a separate sentence for each condition | "The error fell from ⟨v1⟩ (⟨baseline⟩) to ⟨v2⟩ under ⟨protocol⟩." |
| A judgement with its evidence in one sentence | a short verdict sentence | "The ⟨method⟩ beat a ⟨baseline⟩ of the same size but not a larger one, so size still limited it." |
| A direct statement of a dependence | an embedded question | "⟨Design A⟩ tolerates ⟨X⟩, whereas in ⟨design B⟩ ⟨X⟩ enters the result directly." |
| Length set by content | uniform sentences | a long sentence for a mechanism and its conditions; a short one for a plain result |
| An ending on the last relevant fact | a forecast or a generic "would allow …" sentence | end with the limitation and its scope |
| One citation per study, where its story starts and where its result lands | a citation after every sentence of one study | cite at the first mention and at the result |

## 4. The line of thought in each genre

The chain of needs is the same everywhere; what creates the need differs by genre.

| Genre and section | What creates the next need | Typical chain |
|---|---|---|
| Review body | the field's unsolved problem | problem → who tried what and why → result with its condition → what it settled and what it left → the next work |
| Article Introduction | a gap that the paper closes | what is known → the specific limit → why existing approaches cannot remove it → what this work does |
| Article Results | an observation that needs explaining or testing | what we measured and why → what we saw → the control or comparison that rules out the obvious alternative → what it establishes → the next measurement |
| Article Discussion | the reach of the result | main finding restated as a claim → the mechanism and its evidence → the comparison with prior work at the same scope → the limit and the experiment that would remove it |
| Methods | reproducibility | plain, ordered, complete; the line of thought matters little here, and the regularities of section 1 do no harm |
| Abstract | the decision a reader makes | context in one sentence → gap → approach → main quantitative result with its condition → what it enables |
| Proposal aims and rationale | the reviewer's doubt about feasibility | the gap at field level → the hypothesis → preliminary evidence that makes it credible → the aim → the expected outcome and the fallback |
| Thesis chapter | the question the previous chapter left | the question → the approach → the results → what the next chapter takes up |
| Response to reviewers | the referee's point | the point restated briefly → what was changed or why not → where the change is (page, line) |
| Captions and table notes | the reader's need to read the float alone | what is shown → conditions and evidence levels → abbreviations |

## 5. What stays out of the text

- Questions of any kind: rhetorical questions, embedded questions as sentence openers ("How …", "Why …", "Whether …", "Which …") and question headings. Write the answer.
- Thinking aloud: "the question is", "the key is", "what matters here is", "it remains to be seen", "one may ask", "the catch is".
- Commentary on the paragraph or the document ("This raises an important issue", "We now turn to").
- Template angles stamped on every paragraph; use the steps of section 2 only where the material has them.
- Link words added to sentences that do not depend on each other. If no causal, contrastive or time relation exists, the content belongs in a table or a list.
- Uniform causal chains: about seven or eight explanation connectives (so, because, since, then) per 100 sentences are enough. `scripts/prose_lint.py` reports more than 40 per 10,000 words.
- References to visual styling the output does not have ("the shaded row", "values in bold"). Check the built table or figure first.

## 6. Drafting a paragraph from scratch

1. Read the paragraph before and the paragraph after (or the outline points around it), and note privately what each leaves open.
2. List the facts the paragraph must carry: numbers with conditions and evidence labels, citations, float references. Each fact comes from the evidence table, never from memory.
3. Order the facts by the steps of section 2. Drop facts that answer no need; they belong in a table, a figure or another paragraph.
4. Write the paragraph in one go, as a person explaining the work to a colleague in the field. Name actors, use the terms the cited papers use, and put conditions in parentheses.
5. Read it once for rhythm. Merge a run of short sentences that share a subject; split a sentence that carries two claims; leave alone any variation the content creates.
6. Check it against the facts list and run `scripts/prose_lint.py`. Fix `number-run`, `question-voice` and `thinking-aloud` hits; check `ordinal-enumeration`, `aphorism` and the connective advisory by eye.
7. Write the second language from the finished first-language text (bilingual-consistency.md), applying the same rules to its own connectives and rhythm.

## 7. Revising an existing paragraph

1. Keep the facts list fixed: the set of numbers, citation keys and float references must not change.
2. Find the regularity of section 1 that the paragraph shows, and remove it by reordering, not by adding words. Do not add link words to an unchanged list.
3. Rewrite as a whole when the paragraph is an inventory; edit single sentences when only one or two read badly. Paragraphs that read well keep their wording.
4. After rewriting, compare the new version with the old for a new regularity: a connective on every sentence, every sentence the same length, the same opener formula.
5. Table notes: say what columns and labels mean and which scope each value has, in short declarative sentences. Do not restate rows and do not refer to styling the table does not have.

## 8. Section openings

A heading with subsections opens with a two- or three-sentence overview before the first subsection, and sibling headings at the same level do the same. The overview names the physical, methodological or conceptual basis that unites the subsections and says how they divide it. It does not count the subsections or describe the order of the text. Write it like body text, with a concrete subject and a fact in each sentence.

Constructed example: "⟨Section topic⟩ relies on ⟨property⟩ changing with time after an input. ⟨Subsection 1 topic⟩ uses a sharp change at a threshold, and ⟨subsection 2 topic⟩ uses a slow, history-dependent change."

## 9. Depth: what makes the content expert

Prose that avoids every regularity can still be shallow. Depth comes from the content, and it is planned before drafting.

- **Mechanism over outcome.** Say why a result came out as it did (the physical, chemical, biological or statistical cause), with the evidence for that cause.
- **Comparison at the same scope.** A number means something only next to a comparator measured the same way; state both scopes or give no ratio.
- **Conditions that change the answer.** Temperature, sample size, dataset, protocol and accounting boundary go with the number that depends on them.
- **The strongest counter-case.** Mention the result or the regime where the claim fails, with its source.
- **The decisive next result.** End a line of work with the measurement or design that would settle what is open, stated as a fact about the field.
- **Correctness first.** A claim the source does not contain is removed. A smooth sentence built on a wrong number is worse than a plain sentence built on a right one.

## Checklist

- Each sentence answers a need created by the one before it.
- The links come mostly from sentence order and named actors; explanation connectives stay near seven or eight per 100 sentences.
- No run of three bare number sentences; every number has its comparison or meaning, its condition and its evidence label.
- Sentence lengths vary with the content; conditions sit in parentheses.
- No questions, embedded questions, musings or comments on the text.
- Groups are named by what separates them, not counted.
- Judgements carry their evidence and scope in the same sentence.
- The paragraph ends on a fact, not a forecast or a moral.
- Notes and captions mention only styling that the built output has.
- The second language carries the same numbers, citations and float references, with its own natural rhythm.
