# Grant proposals and theses

## Grant proposals

Write the scheme's own section headings exactly; reviewers score against them. Always check the current call document for page limits, sections and scoring criteria before drafting, because schemes change their templates.

### Common structure
1. **Aims.** Two to four aims, each a testable objective with a measurable outcome. One sentence each, then a short paragraph.
2. **Significance and originality.** The problem, why it matters now, what is new relative to named prior work (including the applicant's own).
3. **Preliminary results.** Evidence that the approach works, with figures and numbers traced to papers or data; mark unpublished data as such.
4. **Research plan.** For each aim: method, experiments or simulations, expected results with success criteria, analysis, and how it feeds the next aim.
5. **Risks and alternatives.** For each main risk, the early sign of failure and the fallback route.
6. **Timeline and deliverables.** Gantt-style table by quarter; papers, prototypes, datasets, trained students.
7. **Team and resources.** Why this team and this equipment make the plan feasible.
8. **Budget justification.** Tied to the plan item by item.

### Chinese schemes (e.g. 国家自然科学基金)
Follow the official 正文 outline: 立项依据（研究意义、国内外研究现状及发展动态分析、主要参考文献）; 研究内容、研究目标及拟解决的关键科学问题; 拟采取的研究方案及可行性分析; 本项目的特色与创新之处; 年度研究计划及预期研究结果; 研究基础与工作条件. 关键科学问题 are scientific questions, not engineering tasks; 创新之处 are two to four points, each checkable. Keep 研究内容 and 研究目标 aligned one to one.

### Hong Kong RGC schemes (e.g. GRF, ECS)
Follow the current RGC template (for example: abstract, long-term impact, objectives, background of research, research plan and methodology, pathways to impact, and the applicant's track record). Objectives are numbered and each is addressed by the plan.

### Proposal style
- Every claim of novelty compared with a named reference.
- Every aim ends with the measurement that shows success.
- No reviewer should need to infer why an experiment is in the plan.

## Theses
- Chapter 1: problem, gap, research questions, contributions, thesis structure (one paragraph per chapter).
- Chapter 2: literature review organised around the research questions, not a catalogue; it ends with the gaps each later chapter addresses.
- Chapters 3–N: each chapter reads as a paper (introduction, methods, results, discussion, summary) but begins with a paragraph linking it to the previous chapter and ends with a summary that sets up the next.
- Final chapter: answers to the research questions, limitations, future work with concrete next steps.
- Keep one terminology table and one symbol list across chapters; number equations and figures by chapter (Fig. 3.4).
- Reuse published papers with the university's rules on prior publication and co-authorship statements.
