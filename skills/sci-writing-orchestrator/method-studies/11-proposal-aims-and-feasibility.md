# Case 11: a grant proposal built from the reviewer's doubts

A method study for research proposals: national science foundations, regional research councils, fellowships and thesis proposals. Scheme rules (page limits, section names, budget forms) come from the call text and override everything here.

## The reader and the decision
A panel member reads many proposals against fixed criteria, usually significance, originality, feasibility and the team's track record. Each criterion raises a doubt that the text removes before the reviewer forms it. Write those doubts down first, each as the statement the proposal has to prove:
- **Significance:** the problem is real and matters beyond this group.
- **Originality:** the idea is new against the best named alternative.
- **Feasibility:** this team can do it in this time with these resources.
- **Risk:** the project still delivers if the central hypothesis is wrong.

## Order of drafting
1. **One-sentence objective** with the measurable outcome: "⟨Capability⟩ at ⟨level⟩ under ⟨condition⟩ by ⟨end of project⟩."
2. **Aims**, two to four, each ending with the measurement that shows success and the criterion that would count as failure.
3. **Preliminary evidence** for each aim: the group's own data or published results that make the aim feasible, with conditions and evidence labels.
4. **Risks and fallbacks** per aim: the most likely failure, the early signal of it, and the alternative route.
5. **Background and significance** last, cut to what the aims need. A background longer than the plan is the most common weakness.
6. **Summary or abstract** written from the finished aims.

## The chain inside each section
| Section | Chain |
|---|---|
| rationale | gap at field level, with the best current numbers → why existing approaches stop at that level (mechanism) → the hypothesis that removes the stop |
| aim | aim → hypothesis → approach (key experiment, controls, sample size or power) → expected outcome with the success criterion → fallback |
| preliminary evidence | what was done → what was seen, with the number and condition → what it makes feasible → what it leaves for the project |
| work plan | tasks in dependency order, each with a deliverable and a month |
| team | each member's specific contribution to a named aim, with one supporting output |

Every claim of novelty names the reference it is new against. Every experiment states why it is in the plan; a reviewer should never have to infer it.

## Style for proposals
- First person plural and future tense for the plan ("we will measure"), past tense for preliminary results.
- Short paragraphs with a bold lead phrase are acceptable where the scheme's template invites them, but each paragraph still follows a chain of needs.
- Promises become deliverables: "a valuable resource" becomes "a dataset of ⟨n⟩ ⟨items⟩ released under ⟨licence⟩".
- No defensive sentences about what has not been done. The gap is stated at field level and becomes the aim.
- In Chinese schemes the same chain applies to 立项依据, 研究内容, 研究目标, 拟解决的关键科学问题 and 研究方案. Write the key scientific problem as a statement of what is unknown and why it blocks the objective (bilingual-consistency.md).

## Failure modes
| Failure | Repair |
|---|---|
| aims that are tasks ("characterise", "investigate") | state the hypothesis and the measurable outcome |
| preliminary data without conditions | add the condition and evidence label, as in a paper |
| no fallback | name the most likely failure and the alternative route |
| significance written as importance adjectives | state who can do what afterwards that they cannot do now |
| budget not tied to aims | each major item justified by the aim and task that need it |

## Checks
- Each aim has a success criterion, a failure criterion, preliminary evidence and a fallback.
- The objective, aims and summary carry the same numbers.
- Every reference to the group's own work is checkable (DOI or preprint) and correctly labelled as published or submitted.
