# Case 10: Introductions, abstracts and titles written from the reader's decision

A method study for the parts of a paper that editors and reviewers read first. It applies to articles, letters, reviews and perspectives in any field.

## Start from the decision
Write down, before any sentence, the decision a reader makes after the first page:
- an editor: whether to send the paper out for review;
- a reviewer: whether the claim is new, supported and of the right scope;
- a reader in the field: whether to read on, cite it or change a method.

Then write the main claim in one sentence with its number and condition. Every opening paragraph exists to make that sentence supported and necessary.

## The Introduction as a chain of needs
| Step | Content | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| known | what the field can do now, stated with the best current numbers and their conditions | "In recent years, X has attracted much attention" |
| specific limit | the one limit this paper addresses, named in measurable terms | a list of five problems, four of which the paper does not touch |
| why existing approaches cannot remove it | the mechanism behind the limit, with the strongest attempt and why it stops | dismissing prior work without numbers |
| this work | "Here, we …" with what was done, the main result and its condition | a promise ("paves the way") instead of a result |

Each step creates the need for the next. In a review, the last step becomes the scope and organising axis of the review instead of "here, we".

## Abstracts at three lengths
Write the long version first, then cut. Each version keeps the main number with its condition and qualifier.
- **About 250 words:** context (one sentence) → gap → approach → main result with condition → second result or mechanism → what it enables, stated as a capability with its limit.
- **About 150 words:** context and gap in one sentence → approach → main result with condition → one sentence on the capability.
- **About 50 words (key points, TOC text, highlights):** the result and the condition. No background.

An abstract sentence that a reader cannot check against a figure or a table is cut. Abbreviations are defined in the abstract even when they are defined again in the text.

## Titles
- A noun phrase that names the object and the result, or a short claim when one finding carries the paper.
- Test each word: remove it and see whether the title loses information. Words such as "novel", "high-performance" and "towards" usually fail the test.
- A title promises the scope; check that the paper delivers it at that scope (a material, a device, a system, a population).

## Field variations
| Field or venue | What changes |
|---|---|
| broad journals (Nature-family, Science) | the first paragraph is written for a scientist outside the field; the gap has to matter beyond it |
| society journals | the field's own terms can be used from the first sentence; the comparison with the best prior result carries more weight |
| clinical | structured abstracts (background, methods, results, conclusions); registration number; the primary endpoint first |
| computer science conferences | contributions as a short bulleted list are expected; still give the main number with its benchmark and setting |
| humanities-influenced social science | the research question may be stated as a question in the Introduction by convention; follow the venue, and keep the prose free of musing |

## Checks
- The main claim appears with the same number and qualifier in the title (if numeric), the abstract, the end of the Introduction and the conclusion.
- The Introduction cites the strongest current competitor as well as older work.
- No sentence of the Introduction would fit unchanged into another paper on a different topic.
